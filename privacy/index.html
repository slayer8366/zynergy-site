Privacy Policy for Forager

Last updated: 9 November 2026  ·  Applies to the Forager Android app (com.zynergylabs.forager.app) on Google Play.

This is the document Google Play's Data safety declaration points at. It is written to match what the app's code actually does.

The short version

Forager has no account, no sign-in, no sync and no analytics. Nothing you record, meaning tracks, journal entries, photos, waypoints, planned trips and their routes, offline map regions and settings, is uploaded to us or to anyone else.

What is transmitted, while your phone is online, is where you are looking: the coordinates a search runs on, the map tiles for the area on screen, the map tiles for an area you are choosing to download, and the edges of any area you download for offline use. Those go to iNaturalist, Open-Meteo, the map tile providers, and our map server on Cloudflare. They carry a location and your IP address, and they are logged by the services that receive them.

Because location leaves the device and reaches third parties, the Play Data safety declaration for this app states that location is collected and shared. That is the accurate description, not a conservative one.

What stays on your device

Stored in the app's own private storage, readable by no other app:

  • Recorded tracks and their individual GPS points: latitude, longitude, timestamp, elevation, accuracy, speed, speed accuracy, and air pressure from the phone’s barometer where it has one.
  • Journal and log entries, including any coordinate attached to a find.
  • Photos taken in the app or imported from your gallery.
  • Waypoints, and planned trips. A trip can carry a route you plot: each stop’s name and position, the waypoint or trip a stop came from, and the path between the stops. The route is worked out on the phone, from trail route data already downloaded; plotting it sends nothing.
  • Downloaded offline map regions, with the trail routes and the extra map detail stored for each region.
  • Preferences such as units and map mode.

None of this is transmitted by the app. It is deleted when you uninstall Forager or clear its data.

Android's own backup is switched off

The app sets android:allowBackup="false", which turns off both Android Auto Backup and the device-to-device transfer that runs during a new phone's setup. Your phone therefore does not copy Forager's data to your Google account, and does not hand it to another device on your behalf.

Moving your data belongs to the app rather than to the operating system, so Forager has its own Backup, in Tools, then Settings. “Back up now” writes a single file, containing your tracks, journal entries, waypoints, planned trips with their routes, and photos, to a place you choose with Android’s own file picker. “Automatic backup” can repeat this daily, weekly or monthly into a folder you choose. The file is not encrypted and goes only where you put it: if you choose a cloud folder, that service’s own terms apply to it. “Restore from backup” reads a file you pick and either replaces what is on the phone or merges it in. Forager never sends a backup anywhere on its own.

You can also share a recorded track as a GPX file. The file holds the track’s points and its waypoints, and goes only to the app or place you choose in Android’s share sheet. Forager does not send it anywhere itself.

As with any HTTP request, each of these also reveals your IP address and ordinary request metadata to the recipient. Each recipient's own privacy policy governs what it then does with that.

One thing the app does not request itself: tapping an observation to open it on iNaturalist hands a link to your browser or to the iNaturalist app. From that point it is an ordinary web visit made by that other app, under its own terms, not a request Forager makes.

While you choose an area in Offline maps, and the phone has signal, the app asks our map server for the map tiles that cover the area under the pin, at a coarse zoom, to check whether Forager Trail Map has full detail across it. It asks again each time the pin stops or the radius changes, and when the signal returns. This happens before you download anything, including when a trip’s Download Maps opens the page with the trip’s pin already set. The answers are kept in the app’s memory until it closes, and are not stored.

Downloading an offline region issues a large number of tile requests to our map server in a short time, covering the whole region. That is the download. The app then asks our map server for that region’s trail routes: one request carrying the region’s edges, then one request per route file, each carrying the same edges. Every one of these route requests carries the app key. If the route data on the server is replaced between the first request and the files, the app asks once more, the same way. It also asks, once each, for detailed map tiles the download didn’t include, which the way to a waypoint reads offline; these carry tile coordinates only, with no key. It asks again for a region whose routes or map detail are missing when you open Offline maps with signal, when the signal returns while Offline maps is on screen, or when you tap “Try again”. Once a region is downloaded, the map inside it can be drawn with no connection at all. Searching still makes requests.

Our map server on Cloudflare

Our map server, named above, is a Cloudflare Worker that we operate on Cloudflare's platform, serving the map and the trail route data out of a Cloudflare R2 bucket. Because we run it ourselves, no third-party map vendor sees the requests your device makes to it.

We keep no request logs. Request logging is explicitly disabled in the server’s configuration rather than left to a platform default, and we confirmed in Cloudflare’s dashboard that it reports logging off.

The server’s own code writes a few warning and error lines when its own data is missing, damaged or misconfigured. What they can carry is about the server’s own data: the names of its data files, their sizes and positions inside them, the build of the route data, and route squares of five degrees, with, for a damaged route file, the one-degree cell inside it. None of them carries an IP address, the app key, a tile or an area’s edges. With logging off, these lines are not stored anywhere. Only we can see them, live, in a viewing session while one is open; Cloudflare documents that such a session also shows each request as it arrives. Nothing from a viewing session is kept.

No per-request log is kept by any other route either. We have not set up any export of the server’s request events, and the request records Cloudflare keeps for our website’s own domain do not cover the map server’s address. Cloudflare’s metrics for the server show aggregate request counts, not individual requests.

To limit route requests, the server counts them in Cloudflare’s rate-limiting counters, over a one-minute period: 30 a minute from one IP address, and 600 a minute in all on the one app key that every copy of the app shares, which counts the app’s traffic, not any person’s. The counts are how the limits work; they are not a list of requests.

Cloudflare operates the infrastructure and, as the host, processes these requests on our behalf under its own privacy policy. We state no retention period for request records here because there is no setting of ours that produces one, and we will not publish a figure we cannot verify.

What our settings do keep for a set time is content, not records of requests. The server keeps copies of what it serves in Cloudflare’s cache, at the location that served them, so the same thing asked for again is not read or cut again: map tiles and the map’s tile descriptions for up to a day, under their tile address; the map’s fonts and icons for up to seven days; and a trail-route file cut for an area for up to 30 days, under an address that includes the area’s edges. Each copy is kept under its own address, and none records who asked for it or when.

Nothing about these requests is linked to an account, because the app has none. Nothing is sold, shared for advertising, or used for profiling.

Photos and location metadata

Photos stay on your phone. The app never transmits one. What the app does with the location metadata embedded in a photo file is narrower than it may sound, and is stated here as it actually is:

  • For a photo taken inside Forager, the app's own camera writes it into Forager's private storage and attaches no location to it. Before the photo is stored, Forager removes its embedded metadata, including GPS tags, timestamps, camera make and model, and thumbnails, keeping only its orientation, its colour profile and what is needed to display it. If that step cannot complete, the photo is stored as it was, with its metadata, rather than lost.
  • When the camera is set to RAW, Forager also stores the sensor’s raw image as a DNG file beside the photo. Forager writes that file itself, and writes no location, time, or camera make or model into it. It keeps the sensor’s calibration data, which describes how the sensor records colour. If Forager cannot confirm the file is clean of those details, the RAW file is deleted and the photo is kept as a JPG. RAW files are included in backup, restore, export and delete, like the photo they belong to.
  • For a gallery import, Forager does not run a metadata-stripping step over the copy it stores. On Android 10 and later, the platform itself redacts GPS tags from the copy the app reads. On Android 9 and earlier, which the app still supports, there is no such redaction, and an imported photo's copy can keep its embedded GPS tags.

This matters if you export or share a photo yourself. Depending on your Android version and how the photo got into the app, it may carry the coordinates of where it was taken. Treat a shared photo the way you would treat a shared track.

A find's coordinate, when the app records one, is stored in the app's own database rather than read out of a camera capture's EXIF.

Permissions and what they are used for

  • Location (ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION, ACCESS_COARSE_LOCATION, FOREGROUND_SERVICE_LOCATION): showing your position, recording a track, navigating back, and the coordinates a species or weather lookup searches on. While the app is in the foreground it receives location updates continuously, not only while you are recording. When you leave the app, it releases its location subscription rather than relying on Android to withhold it.
  • Camera (CAMERA): Forager's own camera, for taking photos to attach to a journal entry. The app asks for it when you first open the camera, not at launch; if you decline, the camera does not open, and you can still attach photos from your gallery. Photos it takes are stored on your device, as described under "Photos and location metadata" above. Choosing a photo from your gallery needs no permission: it uses Android's photo picker.
  • ACCESS_MEDIA_LOCATION: reading the capture date and coordinate of a photo you import, so a find can be dated and placed. Read separately from the stored copy's bytes.
  • Notifications, vibrate, foreground service: the off-track alert, the sundown alerts (a turnaround warning and one at sunset, while a track is recording), and the recording notification. The sundown alerts are computed on the device from the clock and your position; nothing is sent anywhere to produce them.
  • Internet: the requests listed above.

Forager does not ask for background location. The permission Android calls ACCESS_BACKGROUND_LOCATION is not requested and not declared. A recording keeps running with your screen off because it runs as a foreground service, with the ongoing notification you can see, not because the app can read your location in the background.

Outside those two states, app open or a recording running, Forager receives no location at all.

You can revoke location permission at any time in Android Settings. Recording will not work without it, but the rest of the app will. Turning it off stops the app using your position; the map still loads tiles for the area on screen while you are online.

No analytics, no ads, no tracking

The app bundles no analytics, crash-reporting or advertising SDK. There is no telemetry of any kind: no usage events, no session data, no device fingerprint, no third-party identifier. Nothing about your use of the app is reported to us unless you write it in a message and send it yourself.

The app writes diagnostic lines to the phone’s own system log, some with a coarse map position; they stay on the phone.

Children

Forager is not directed at children and collects nothing about anyone, including children, beyond what is described above.

Your control over your data

Everything Forager stores is on your device, so you control it directly:

  • Delete individual items in the app: journal entries, photos, recorded tracks, waypoints, offline map regions and planned trips. A trip’s route goes with it.
  • Clear all data through Android Settings → Apps → Forager → Storage → Clear data.
  • Uninstall to remove everything, including the database and the photo files. Because Android backup is switched off for this app, there is no copy in your Google account to survive and reappear.

A backup file you made with Forager’s Backup is yours: it stays wherever you saved it, and you delete it there.

We hold no copy of your data, and no logs of your map requests, so there is nothing for us to produce or delete on your behalf, and no deletion request to make.

Step-by-step detail on deleting everything is at zynergy-labs.com/delete-data, the page named in the Play Data safety declaration.
